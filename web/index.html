A not-held order hands somebody else discretion over the time and price of your trade. For a century the only thing protecting the client was a promise — the best-execution obligation. NEAR Intents signs the economics of every fill, so on NEAR that promise can be a number instead.

The whole mechanism is one expression

No oracle, by construction

Both USD marks are inside the signed payload. No price feed, no pool reserves, no RPC call, no cross-contract call. That is the only reason one verifier can cover every asset and chain Intents lists.

Nothing left to enumerate

Slippage, the bridge fee, the solver's spread and any app fee have already moved those numbers before the venue signed them. So there is no policy grab-bag and no list to keep current.

The counterparty's own signature

The novel move, and the only one: use the counterparty's signature as evidence against the counterparty. No oracle, no friendly signer, nobody to trust.

What the signature actually covers

This is the distinction the whole design turns on. Two fields that look like they belong to the quote are not covered by the venue's signature at all — and a third is signed but chosen by your counterparty, which is just as bad. Hallmark reads none of the three.

appFees is capped at 500 bps and split 50/50 with 1Click, so a third party's true maximum take is 250. The contract cannot read it — and prices it to the basis point anyway, because its effect is already inside the signed amountOutUsd. The malicious fixture costs 503 bps even honoured in full, which is exactly the 500 bps of fees requested on it.
depositAddress is assigned after signing, which killed an entire earlier design: a vault that sent the owner's funds to a filler-nominated address could simply be robbed. The fix was to invert the order of operations so the filler delivers first — §2.4, the load-bearing decision.

Why the promise needs replacing, and why now

The regulator of the primitive has an open docket on exactly this failure.

On 2026-07-24 FINRA issued Regulatory Notice 26-15, a request for comment on modernising best-execution guidance under Rule 5310. Comments closed 2026-09-25. Section 7 raises precisely two concerns about not-held orders:

  1. broad or “blanket” not-held provisions in retail account agreements;
  2. cases where a retail customer's held order is later routed as not held.

Both are the same failure: the client's protection lives in prose somebody else drafted, so it can be applied wholesale, or re-labelled in transit. FINRA also asks whether safe harbors under Rule 5310 would help.

Separately, on 2026-06-11 the SEC proposed Regulation NMS amendments including rescinding Rule 611 — which puts in question the NBBO benchmark the promise was measured against in the first place.

A provable bound is what a safe harbor looks like when it is engineered instead of legislated. That is the thesis, and FINRA asking the question is the tell.

The pattern: every failure refuses, and every refusal is typed. There is no path in which uncertainty results in a transfer. When 1Click went down for forty minutes on 2026-11-02, nothing could be filled and the vault kept refusing exactly as it would have anyway — liveness loss, never a funds loss.

The bug we shipped, and then found

v1 was live on mainnet with a hole that defeated the headline claim.

The cost bound is denominated in USD marks that were true when the venue signed. v1's only freshness check was the quote's own deadline — and the deadline is chosen by whoever asks for the quote. Measured live: 1Click will sign one 3,650 days out, on a free dry:true request.

So a filler could mint a library of free signed quotes, hold them as options on the owner's escrow, and exercise whichever the market later made profitable. The ceiling would pass every time, priced against a market that no longer existed.

The fix used a field that was already inside the signature and simply never read: timestamp. Mandates now carry max_quote_age_secs, checked against it.

Being signed and being trustworthy are different properties. timestamp is written by the venue; deadline is written by the counterparty. Conflating the two is how this shipped — and it is the same reasoning that already rejected depositAddress, applied one level deeper than v1 applied it.

What this is not

Stating the limits is the most credible thing on this page, so they are here rather than at the bottom of a document nobody opens.

Not best execution.

Hallmark proves a bound and enforces a realised cost, not optimality. With one filler and first-come-first-served, the filler delivers near the floor and keeps the spread: the owner gets their ceiling, not the best price in the market. An auction window is the documented next version.

Not an independent price benchmark.

Killed by measurement. Of 8,992 Rhea pools, 31 hold Intents-USDC and zero hold Intents-USDC and wNEAR together — omni-bridge assets and native assets are different tokens with the same tickers. The signed USD marks are the reference because there is no on-chain alternative.

Not a custodian, ever.

The output is in the vault before the input is credited to anyone, and payouts are pull rather than push. No design that involved holding a counterparty's funds survived review.

Not safe against a stolen venue key.

If 1Click's signing key is compromised, an attacker can mint arbitrarily bad signed quotes and the guarantee degrades from cryptographic to “the ceiling the owner signed”. Unmitigated, and not fixable from inside the design.
